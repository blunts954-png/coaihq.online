## SAMPLE CASE 1: SECURITY DEPOSIT DISPUTE (STRONG)

**Case Snapshot:**
- **User:** Sierra Martinez, 34, Bakersfield resident
- **Issue:** Security deposit withheld
- **Amount:** $1,200
- **Timeline:** 25 days since move-out
- **Case Strength:** STRONG

**User's Submission:**

> "My landlord kept my $1,200 deposit almost 4 weeks after I moved out. She said the apartment had 'damage' but won't tell me what the damage was or how much each thing costs. I have photos from move-out day showing the apartment was clean—no holes in walls, no stains, everything working. I texted her 3 times asking for an itemized list and she just says 'I'll send it soon' but never does. I need that money for my new place."

**AI Analysis Output:**

```
Your Situation:
Landlord withheld $1,200 security deposit 25 days after move-out without providing itemized deductions. You have photographic evidence of clean condition at move-out.

California Law Applies:
Cal. Civil Code § 1950.7 (Security Deposits—Landlord Obligations)

Your Legal Position: STRONG

Why:
California law requires landlords to return deposits WITH itemized deductions within 21 days of move-out. Your landlord has violated both requirements: (1) missed the 21-day deadline, and (2) failed to provide itemization. You have photographic evidence supporting your claim of clean condition. Under § 1950.7, you likely qualify for statutory penalties of up to 2x the deposit amount ($2,400 total recovery).

What You Should Do (this week):
1. TODAY: Send the demand letter below via certified mail (keep receipt)
2. TOMORROW: File small claims case at Kern County Superior Court ($30-$75 fee)
3. THIS WEEK: Organize all evidence (photos, lease, texts, certified mail receipt)
4. NEXT WEEK: Prepare for small claims hearing (typically scheduled 2-4 weeks out)

[Draft demand letter provided]

Next Step:
Your case is clear-cut under California law. Connect with a local tenant advocate to review your evidence and ensure you're fully prepared for small claims court.
```

**Why This Is a Quality Lead:**
- ✅ Clear legal violation (21-day deadline missed)
- ✅ Documentary evidence (photos, texts)
- ✅ Motivated client (needs money for new place)
- ✅ Straightforward case (no complex issues)
- ✅ High recovery potential ($1,200 deposit + up to $2,400 penalties)

**Expected Paralegal Value:**

## SAMPLE CASE 2: RETALIATORY EVICTION (STRONG)

**Case Snapshot:**
- **User:** David Rodriguez, 42, Bakersfield resident
- **Issue:** Eviction notice after complaint
- **Rent:** $1,400/month
- **Timeline:** 3-day notice received 1 week ago
- **Case Strength:** STRONG

**User's Submission:**

> "I've been living in my apartment for 3 years, always paid rent on time. Last month I complained to my landlord about mold in the bathroom (sent photos and everything). She said she'd fix it but never did. Two weeks later, she gave me a 3-day notice to vacate or she'll evict me. The notice says 'lease violation' but doesn't say what I violated. I think she's trying to get rid of me because I complained. Is this legal?"

**AI Analysis Output:**

```
Your Situation:

California Law Applies:
Cal. Civil Code § 1942.5 (Retaliatory Eviction)

Your Legal Position: STRONG

Why:
California law prohibits retaliatory eviction within 180 days of a tenant complaint about habitability issues. The timing here is highly suspicious: you complained about mold, then received an eviction notice 2 weeks later. Unless the landlord has a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason (which they must prove), this appears to violate § 1942.5. Additionally, the 3-day notice is invalid if it doesn't specify the lease violation.

What You Should Do (this week):
1. TODAY: DO NOT move out. Stay in your apartment.
2. TODAY: Document everything (save all texts, emails, photos of mold, copy of 3-day notice)
3. TOMORROW: Send written response to landlord stating you believe this is retaliatory (see letter below)
4. THIS WEEK: Contact tenant rights attorney or legal aid (many offer free eviction defense consultations)
5. IF LANDLORD FILES UNLAWFUL DETAINER: You have 5 days to respond—do NOT miss this deadline

Next Step:
```

- ✅ Clear retaliation pattern (complaint → eviction)
- ✅ Strong tenant history (3 years, no late payments)
- ✅ Invalid 3-day notice (no specific violation listed)

## SAMPLE CASE 3: ILLEGAL RENT INCREASE (MODERATE)

**Case Snapshot:**
- **User:** Jessica Chen, 29, Bakersfield resident
- **Issue:** Excessive rent increase
- **Current Rent:** $1,100/month
- **Proposed Increase:** $1,265/month (15% increase)
- **Case Strength:** MODERATE

**User's Submission:**

> "My landlord just gave me a notice that my rent is going up from $1,100 to $1,265 starting next month. That's a 15% increase! I've been here for 2 years and this seems way too high. I thought California had rent control laws? The building was built in 1998. Can they really raise rent this much?"

**AI Analysis Output:**

```
Your Situation:
Landlord proposed 15% rent increase on property built in 1998. You've been a tenant for 2 years.

California Law Applies:
California AB 1482 (Tenant Protection Act of 2019)

Your Legal Position: MODERATE

Why:
Under AB 1482, landlords in California can only raise rent by 5% + local CPI (consumer price index) or 10%, whichever is lower. A 15% increase exceeds this limit. HOWEVER, AB 1482 only applies to properties built more than 15 years ago (your building qualifies—built in 1998). The key question: Does your building qualify for AB 1482 protection? Some exemptions exist (e.g., single-family homes with certain ownership structures, condos). You need to verify whether your building is exempt.

What You Should Do (this week):
1. TODAY: Check if your building is exempt from AB 1482 (see checklist below)
2. THIS WEEK: If not exempt, send letter to landlord citing AB 1482 and refusing the increase
3. THIS WEEK: Check Kern County's local rent control ordinances (some cities have additional protections)
4. NEXT WEEK: If landlord insists on illegal increase, file complaint with California Department of Consumer Affairs

[Exemption checklist provided]
[Draft letter provided]

Next Step:
This situation requires research into your specific building's status. Consult a tenant rights advocate to determine if AB 1482 applies and whether the increase is legal.
```

**Why This Is a Quality Lead:**
- ✅ Clear potential violation (15% > 10% max under AB 1482)
- ✅ Tenant willing to fight (researching laws)
- ✅ Moderate complexity (requires legal analysis)
- ⚠️ Possible exemptions (needs investigation)

## SAMPLE CASE 4: HABITABILITY ISSUE (MODERATE)

**Case Snapshot:**
- **User:** Michael Thompson, 51, Bakersfield resident
- **Issue:** Broken heater in winter
- **Timeline:** 3 weeks without heat
- **Case Strength:** MODERATE to STRONG

**User's Submission:**

> "It's been freezing at night (40s) and my heater broke 3 weeks ago. I told my landlord the day it stopped working. He said he'd send someone but no one ever came. I've texted him 5 times. My kids are getting sick from the cold. I'm thinking about just withholding rent until he fixes it. Can I do that?"

**AI Analysis Output:**

```
Your Situation:
Heater broken for 3 weeks during winter (nighttime temps in 40s). Landlord notified multiple times but has not repaired.

California Law Applies:
Cal. Civil Code § 1941 (Habitability—Landlord Repair Obligations)

Your Legal Position: MODERATE to STRONG

Why:
California law requires landlords to provide adequate heating. A broken heater in winter is a habitability violation under § 1941. You notified the landlord (documented via texts) and gave reasonable time to repair (3 weeks is more than enough). You have the right to "repair and deduct" (pay for repair yourself and deduct from rent) OR withhold rent until repaired. HOWEVER, you must follow the correct legal process—you can't just stop paying rent without notice.

What You Should Do (this week):
1. TODAY: Send written notice (email + text) stating heater is uninhabitable under § 1941 and you will exercise repair-and-deduct OR withhold rent (choose one)
2. IF REPAIR-AND-DEDUCT: Get 2-3 quotes from HVAC contractors, choose cheapest, deduct cost from next rent payment (max 1 month's rent)
3. IF RENT WITHHOLDING: Open escrow account, deposit rent there (proves you're not just skipping payment), notify landlord you're withholding until repair
4. THIS WEEK: Document everything (photos of broken heater, thermostat readings, texts to landlord)

[Draft notice letter provided]
[Repair-and-deduct instructions provided]

Next Step:
Habitability cases are time-sensitive, especially with children in the home. Consult a tenant advocate to ensure you follow the correct legal process for repair-and-deduct or rent withholding.
```

**Why This Is a Quality Lead:**
- ✅ Clear habitability violation (broken heater = unsafe)
- ✅ Well-documented (texts, timeline)
- ✅ Sympathetic facts (kids getting sick)
- ✅ Multiple legal remedies available
- ⚠️ Tenant needs guidance (can't just stop paying rent)

**Expected Paralegal Value:**
- Legal review + strategy: $200-$400
- Ongoing representation if landlord retaliates
- Possible counterclaim for damages (medical bills, hotel costs)

## SAMPLE CASE 5: WEAK CASE (EXAMPLE OF HONESTY)

**Case Snapshot:**
- **User:** Amanda Lee, 27, Bakersfield resident
- **Issue:** Early lease termination
- **Case Strength:** WEAK

**User's Submission:**

> "I signed a 12-month lease but I need to move out after 6 months because I got a job in another city. My landlord says I have to pay rent for the remaining 6 months even though I'm not living there. That's like $7,200! Can I break the lease without paying?"

**AI Analysis Output:**

```
Your Situation:
You want to terminate a 12-month lease early (after 6 months) due to job relocation. Landlord is demanding rent for remaining 6 months.

California Law Applies:
Cal. Civil Code § 1951.2 (Lease Break—Landlord's Duty to Mitigate)

Your Legal Position: WEAK (but with possible defenses)

Why:
California law allows tenants to break leases, but you remain financially responsible for rent until: (1) the landlord finds a new tenant, OR (2) the lease term ends—whichever comes first. However, landlords have a legal duty to "mitigate damages" by making reasonable efforts to re-rent the unit. If your landlord doesn't try to re-rent, you may be able to reduce what you owe. That said, you likely can't eliminate your financial obligation entirely.

What You Should Do (this week):
1. TODAY: Review your lease for early termination clauses or subletting provisions
2. THIS WEEK: Ask landlord (in writing) what efforts they've made to re-rent the unit
3. THIS WEEK: Check if the unit is listed for rent online (Zillow, Craigslist, Apartments.com)
4. IF LANDLORD HAS NOT LISTED: You may have leverage to negotiate a reduced settlement
5. IF LANDLORD IS ACTIVELY RE-RENTING: Offer to help find replacement tenant (speeds up process)

[Draft letter requesting mitigation efforts provided]

Next Step:
This is not a straightforward case. You likely owe something, but the amount is negotiable depending on your landlord's mitigation efforts. Consult a tenant rights attorney to review your lease and assess your options for minimizing financial liability.
```

**Why We're Honest About Weak Cases:**
- ✅ Builds trust (we don't give false hope)
- ✅ User still gets value (knows realistic expectations)
- ✅ Provider sees we filter intelligently (not sending junk leads)
- ✅ Possible settlement negotiation work (still billable for paralegal)

**Expected Paralegal Value:**
- Lease review: $100-$200
- Negotiation letter: $150-$250
- Lower value, but shows lead quality curation
